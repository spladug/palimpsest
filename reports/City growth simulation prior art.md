# Prior art solves pieces, never the whole town

Every subsystem in the design has usable academic precedent, but the research found no published system that combines them, and several of the design's core mechanics have no generative precedent at all. Trail models (Helbing, Goldstone, Physarum) work on rasters or fixed graphs. GPS map-inference algorithms already solve the raster-to-vector-graph step. Network-investment models (Levinson & Yerra, Raimbault) supply edge upgrade and land-attachment feedback. Conzenian morphology, plot metrology (Slater, Tait) and cadastral-inheritance studies (Ward on Leeds) give empirical rules. However, the notes found **no generative model of the burgage cycle, no computational model that grows open fields and then inherits them as urban plots, no parcel system with ghost-boundary preference or split/merge lineage, no ABM with a decomposable bundle of rights and regime-change events, no ejido or railroad-platting ABM, and no engine that runs planned and emergent growth together**. The closest whole-town precedents are a graphics simulation of fortified cities over centuries (Mas, Martin & Patow 2020) and a regional ABM of medieval settlement nucleation (SimFeodal), and neither has plot geometry dynamics. The project's defensible novelty is integration plus three mechanics: trail wear feeding an incremental vector graph in one loop, field-strip-to-burgage inheritance, and lineage-aware plots with persistent ghost boundaries. A further gap is evaluation: procedural-city research rarely validates against urban morphometrics, and the design could be the first to do so systematically. These absence claims come from about 15 to 18 searches per topic and are suggestive, not conclusive.

**How to read the verification markers.** The six source notes used slightly different labels, which are normalised here. **V** means the citation (title, authors, venue) was confirmed by a search result during research. **V~** means the work's existence and title were confirmed, but some details (DOI, pages, issue, author list, or the content summary) come from memory, an abstract or a search snippet only; the cell says which. **M** means the citation is from the researcher's memory and was not re-checked, so double-check it before formal citation. Works the user already knows (Weber/Müller/Gross 2009; Emilien et al 2012; Vanegas et al 2012 parcels; Parish & Müller 2001; Strano et al 2012; Barthélemy & Flammini 2008; Helbing 1997 Nature; Hillier beady ring; Erickson & Lloyd-Jones; SLEUTH; White & Engelen; Batty; Lechner 2003) are excluded except where a follow-up is new.

## Walkers can wear a vector street graph, but no one has closed the loop

The trail literature splits cleanly by representation. The active-walker family keeps state on a continuous ground "comfort" field that grows with footfall and decays exponentially. Only Tero et al's Physarum model works natively on a graph, where it adapts edge conductance to flux ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9806097); [Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1177894)). The design's hybrid of wear raster plus reinforced vector graph therefore matches the field's own division of labour. **The raster settles geometry; the graph settles hierarchy.** The missing piece is extraction, and it turns out to be a solved problem in another field. GPS map inference turns noisy trajectories into road graphs through a pipeline of kernel density, multi-threshold skeletonisation, graph extraction and map-matching. Biagioni & Eriksson's version is almost exactly the wear-raster-to-street-graph step, and its code is public ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F2424321.2424333); [GitHub](https://github.com/haoranshigrab/skeleton_gis2012)). Ahmed et al's benchmark points to **incremental track insertion** as the family that keeps graph identity stable over time. That property matters for a browser simulation in which edges carry names, widths and frontage ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.5138)).

For the edge hierarchy, Levinson & Yerra show that contiguous high-capacity "roads" and hierarchies emerge when links earning more than their upkeep get upgraded and the rest decay. That is a ready rule for track → lane → street, including loss of status ([Transportation Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/trsc.1050.0132)). Raimbault's morphogenesis model couples population density to a growing network through an attachment potential. The potential combines local density, distance to the network, centrality and accessibility, so it is nearly the design's "where do buildings go" rule ([arXiv/UCL](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10073881/1/1805.05195v2.pdf)). At regional scale, archaeology offers White & Barber's "From Everywhere to Everywhere" summed least-cost-path network as a bootstrap that needs no pre-specified origins ([PDF](https://www.colorado.edu/rioverdearchaeology/sites/default/files/attached-files/white_barber_2012.pdf)). Herzog's methodological checklist covers anisotropic slope, neighbourhood artefacts and fords ([Internet Archaeology](https://intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue36/5/index.html)). Geomorphology adds a physical lock-in mechanism: unpaved sunken lanes in the Belgian loess belt incise **1–5 cm/yr**, which over 3–5 centuries yields holloways several metres to tens of metres deep. That figure comes from Belgian loess via a secondary source, so English rates are unverified ([Wikipedia, citing KU Leuven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunken_lane); [Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/574468)). The Verhagen et al review states the niche plainly: few models couple path formation dynamically with settlement ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-04576-0_11)).

## Field systems persist into towns, yet no model generates the hand-off

The historical evidence that rural boundaries become urban boundaries is strong and long-established at two scales. For medieval towns, Conzen's Alnwick founded plan analysis on the principle that plot boundaries are the most persistent plan element ([PDF](https://vitoroliveira.fe.up.pt/alnwick.pdf)). Slater's metrology showed that Stratford-upon-Avon's burgages were fixed in 1196 at **12 × 3.5 perches (198 ft × 57 ft 9 in)** and remain traceable today ([Stratford Society, citing Slater](https://www.stratfordsociety.co.uk/files/History_of_Bridge_Street.pdf)). For the industrial era, Ward showed that Leeds' 19th-century streets and parcels were conditioned by the pre-urban field and estate cadastre, with development filling one field at a time ([AAAG](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8306.1962.tb00402.x)). Beresford's field-by-field history shows back-to-backs fitted to narrow closes ([CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1130000794114984576?lang=en)). French historical GIS on Paris and Tours treats the same phenomenon as the "transmission of forms," with old river channels and rural parcelling as long-lived boundary generators ([Noizet, ENS](https://www.geographie.ens.fr/IMG/file/resilience/journee_etudes_2013/Noizet.pdf)).

On the rural side, the scholarship supports a generator. The hierarchy runs township > fields > furlongs > strips. Headlands sit at furlong ends, and the reverse-S aratral curve comes from turning the ox team before the headland ([Eyre 1955](https://www.medievalists.net/2010/01/the-curving-plough-strip-and-its-historical-implications/)). Furlong orientation follows soils, slope and drainage ([Liddiard, Williamson & Partida](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/36302/)). The dating is contested: Oosthuizen argues for 8th/9th-century proto-layouts ([OAPEN](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/23410)), while the Champion project puts regular open fields in the 11th–12th centuries. The simulation should therefore treat open-field reorganisation as a scenario event rather than a fixed fact. **No computational model was found that generates open fields and then inherits them as urban plots.** The specific mapping of headlands to back lanes and furlong boundaries to cross-streets is plausible and often noted, but the notes retrieved no direct citable evidence for it, nor for surviving reverse-S urban plot boundaries. The one claim that early burgages "were simply the ploughland strips" comes from a low-quality wiki mirror.

## Plots that split, merge and remember have empirical rules but no generator

Conzen's burgage cycle is consistently summarised as four phases: institutive layout, repletive back-land infill, climax saturation, and a recessive drop in coverage, or "urban fallow," before redevelopment ([Sciforum summary](https://sciforum.net/manuscripts/5993/manuscript.pdf)). That is a discrete per-plot state machine. **No generative computational model of it was found.** Metrology supplies hard numbers. Tait found Edinburgh plot widths clustered at a **7.7 m unit with groups at ¾ and 1¼ units**, and Elgin at an **8.1 m unit with 1¼–2 unit plots** ([PSAS](https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/9724); [Elgin](https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/download/9756/9723/9707)). These give multimodal width histograms that a split engine should reproduce. The Scottish evidence also warns that burgage series were often **laid out at varied widths rather than produced by later subdivision of uniform units**, which changes how the initial medieval layout should be generated. Siksna's 12-city comparison shows that oversized blocks are "predictably broken down, through successive uncoordinated actions," with 50–70 m blocks performing best. That is a direct target for the 19th-century US scenario ([Urban Morphology](https://doi.org/10.51347/jum.v1i1.4048)). Whitehand's neighbour-effect hypothesis, supported by recent Chalmers work, makes change depend on "mutual support and interaction between neighbouring plots rather than their individual character." It is the strongest citable rule for split, merge and rebuild probabilities ([Chalmers](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/513622)).

On the algorithmic side, CityEngine's recursive, offset and skeleton subdivision modes are the industry baseline. Skeleton mode maps onto lengthwise burgage cuts and recursive mode onto depthwise back-land splits ([Esri docs](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/2019.0/help/help-layers-block-parameters.htm)). Kelly's mixed weighted straight skeleton allows per-edge weights, and those weights could encode how strongly a ghost boundary attracts a new cut ([Glasgow](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/4975)). Cadastral database research provides the event vocabulary: split, union, reallocation, modification of the common border, and France's division/merge/rectification set. It also supplies the topology checks (extended 4-intersection model) ([ISPRS](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXV/congress/comm2/papers/229.pdf)). **Storing parent and child IDs per event is exactly the lineage store that ghost boundaries need.** None of the graphics papers implements ghost-boundary preference or a split/merge history. Ben-Hamouche's argument that iterated Islamic inheritance subdivision produced the fractal complexity of Muslim cities gives the contrasting tenure case to impartible English burgage tenure in the same engine ([Architexturez](https://architexturez.net/node/215716)).

## Tenure plugins rest on theory and history, not on existing models

Land-market ABMs give tested mechanics for bid/ask formation and speculation, but all treat property as undivided freehold. The ALMA model formalises bilateral bid/ask pricing and division of gains from trade in open-access form ([JASSS](https://www.jasss.org/12/1/3.html)). CHALMS adds farmer holdouts and adaptive developer price expectations, the closest model of speculative lot holding, and its code is on CoMSES ([CoMSES](https://www.comses.net/codebases/3368)). Augustijn-Beckers et al's Dar es Salaam model is **vector-based and micro-scale**, using infill, extension and enlargement rules. Those rules fit encroachment on commons and irregular ejido subdivision ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2011.01.001)). The data model for the rights bundle comes from Schlager & Ostrom's five rights: access, withdrawal, management, exclusion and alienation. Each is held cumulatively by authorised users, claimants, proprietors and owners ([open PDF](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlcrest/api/core/bitstreams/120981f9-4a44-48e2-b930-1c7e726b2eb8/content)). A 2019 critique argues against assuming the rights nest, which supports a non-nested parcel × right → holder matrix in which, for example, a lord holds alienation and a tenant holds use and inheritance ([UEA](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/61963/)). Webster & Lai provide the theory that plot pattern emerges from rights allocation and transaction costs ([Elgar](https://e-elgar.com/shop/gbp/property-rights-planning-and-markets-9781840649048.html)).

The scenario literature is historical rather than computational. For ejidos, Jones & Ward argue that the 1991–92 Article 27 reform was **deregulation, not privatisation**, shifting approval powers from the Agrarian Reform Ministry to social-development and municipal bodies ([IJURR](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2427.00124)). Schumacher et al find incorporation of social land into urban use "far below expectations" ([Land](https://doi.org/10.3390/land8100146)). A regime-change event should therefore move rights between entities and keep conversion slow, patchy and often informal. PROCEDE's three certified categories (parcela, uso común, solar urbano) map directly onto three parcel types ([ERA](https://era.anthropology.ac.uk/Era_Resources/Era/Peasants/mexcase16a.html)). For US railroad towns, Hudson documents **500+ town sites platted 1880–1920 in north-central North Dakota at roughly ten-mile intervals**, with the railroad company as the initial holder of all lots ([Great Plains Quarterly](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/greatplainsquarterly/952)). **No ejido ABM/CA, no railroad-platting ABM, no burgage/open-field/enclosure ABM and no decomposable-bundle ABM were found.** The enclosure and encroachment literature (Hoskins and others) was not verified in this research, which is a hole in the notes rather than evidence of absence.

## Whole-town precedents run on events or networks, not plots

Two works come closest to "a town over centuries." Mas, Martin & Patow step a fortified city through user-set years, with scripted growth, battles and wall events, inverse design, and historian-validated cases at Carcassonne, Girona and Barcelona ([EG](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf13897)). Barthélemy et al's study of Paris from 1789 to 2010 shows that most network measures evolve smoothly. **Haussmann-style planning appears as a reorganisation of betweenness and a shift in block-shape distribution**, which gives a quantitative fingerprint for the planned/emergent mix ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.2203)). For planned templates, Lilley's GIS atlas of Edward I's 13 new towns founded 1277–1303 offers downloadable vector plans ([ADS](https://doi.org/10.5284/1000022)). His Antiquity paper finds shared design principles but no single standard plan ([Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/designs-and-designers-of-medieval-new-towns-in-wales/5F2B4C5ED16C8DA5BFDFC575C3981FFF)). Lilley's argument that surveying improved from the mid-12th century justifies a "technology" parameter that controls layout regularity ([Urban Morphology](https://journal.urbanform.org/index.php/jum/article/view/3880)). **No model was found that simulates a planted framework together with its emergent infill and decay**, and none of bastide build-out or failed plantations.

Rendering received almost no research coverage. The only direct precedent in the notes is Müller et al's shape-grammar reconstruction of Puuc buildings driven by GIS footprints and attributes, which is a single moment in time ([EG](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/VAST.VAST06.139-146)). Recent learned building-layout generators (GlobalMapper, COHO) target modern cities ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.09693)). Accessibility-based land value is supported only indirectly, through Raimbault's attachment potential, Llobera's accessibility surfaces (M), and centrality measures that predict movement (Hillier & Iida, below). Classical land-rent theory was not part of the notes, so this subsystem needs its own follow-up search.

## Morphometrics can make this the first validated procedural town

Procedural-city research mostly validates by eye or by user study, and recent deep generators add distributional distances on modern US data. **None uses the morphometric literature systematically**, which leaves an opening ([COHO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.11294)). The most direct "does this look medieval?" test is Dibble et al's study of 45 cities, in which nine dimensions classify **about 90% of cases by historic origin**, driven mainly by block perimeter, building coverage and regular plot coverage ([Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/61294)). Louf & Barthélemy's area-conditioned block shape factor is pure polygon geometry and simple to compute in JS ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.2094)). Boeing's street-orientation entropy separates grids from organic towns, and Edwardian new towns should score low while market towns score high ([Boeing](https://geoffboeing.com/publications/urban-spatial-order-entropy/)). No JavaScript port of momepy, OSMnx or depthmapX exists, so reference distributions should be computed offline and the formulas reimplemented in the browser. No open dataset of digitised medieval English burgage plots was found, and no published plot-series detection algorithm either.

## Conclusion

The prior art changes the project's framing more than its architecture. Most of the hard technical steps already have proven algorithms in neighbouring fields: GPS map inference for extraction, transport economics for hierarchy, cadastral databases for lineage, land-market ABMs for exchange. So the project's research contribution is not any single mechanism but the **coupling across time scales**: walking wears paths, paths become frontage, frontage sets plot widths inherited from strips, and rights determine who may cut those plots. Each link in that chain is empirically documented but has never been simulated. The strongest publishable claim is probably the field-to-burgage-to-ghost-boundary pipeline, because it turns Conzen, Slater and Ward from descriptions into a testable generator. Lilley's atlas, Tait's metrology and Dibble's classifier provide validation targets that most procedural-city papers lack.

Before claiming novelty, the user should close three specific holes: classical accessibility and land-rent models, enclosure and encroachment history, and 2.5D/3D procedural rendering of vernacular buildings. All three were under-searched rather than found empty. The user should also re-verify every **M** item before citing it, and recover the exact Kwak and Wickramasinghe references, since the brief cites works these searches could not find.
