# Urban form metrics, tools and datasets for validating simulated towns

Scope: quantitative "is this plausible?" checks for a browser-based vector simulation outputting street polylines, parcel polygons and building footprints (default: medieval English market town). Strano et al. 2012 (densification/exploration) is already known and not repeated here.

Verification note: items marked **[verified]** had citation/DOI confirmed via search results this session. Items marked **[from memory]** are well-known references whose DOI was not re-confirmed this session (full-text fetches failed); the writer should spot-check those links.

---

## Q1. Urban morphometrics: core frameworks and tools (Fleischmann, Dibble, Porta, Boeing, Louf & Barthélemy, Barthélemy et al.)

### Takeaway
A mature, citable toolkit exists: momepy (Python) implements most building/plot/street morphometrics. Dibble et al. 2019 show that a handful of measures (block perimeter, building coverage, regular plot coverage) separate medieval from later tissues. Louf & Barthélemy's block area/shape-factor "fingerprint", Boeing's orientation entropy, and Barthélemy et al.'s Paris time series give distribution-level and time-series checks that can be ported to JavaScript.

### Cited Findings
- **Fleischmann, M. (2019). momepy: Urban Morphology Measuring Toolkit. *Journal of Open Source Software*, 4(43), 1807. https://doi.org/10.21105/joss.01807** [verified]. This is the Python (PySAL/GeoPandas) library implementing dimension, shape, spatial-distribution, intensity, connectivity and diversity metrics for buildings, tessellation cells, blocks and streets. Relevance: it is the reference implementation to port, or to run offline on real towns (OSM or OS footprints) to build "target distributions". There is no JS port; individual formulas (e.g. elongation, convexity, circular compactness, street profile, node degree, meshedness) are simple to reimplement with turf.js. — [JOSS](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.01807); [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/70616)
- **Fleischmann, M., Romice, O., & Porta, S. (2021). Measuring urban form: Overcoming terminological inconsistencies for a quantitative and comprehensive morphologic analysis of cities. *Environment and Planning B: Urban Analytics and City Science*, 48(8), 2133–2150. https://doi.org/10.1177/2399808320910444** [verified]. It proposes an "Index of Elements" that classifies urban form characters into six categories (dimension, shape, spatial distribution, intensity, connectivity, diversity) at three scales (small, medium, large). Relevance: use it as a checklist so the validation suite covers every category rather than only street metrics. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/2399808320910444); [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/71670)
- **Fleischmann, M., Feliciotti, A., Romice, O., & Porta, S. (2020). Morphological tessellation as a way of partitioning space: Improving consistency in urban morphology at the plot scale. *Computers, Environment and Urban Systems*, 80, 101441.** [verified title/venue/article number; DOI expected to be 10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2019.101441, from memory]. It derives a "morphological cell" (a Voronoi-like partition seeded by building footprints) as a proxy for plots where cadastral data is missing. Relevance: real English towns rarely have open plot data, so tessellate both real and generated footprints and compare cell-level metrics like for like. This also lets you check whether generated parcels resemble the tessellation-implied ones. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/70666)
- **Fleischmann, M., Feliciotti, A., Romice, O., & Porta, S. (2022). Methodological foundation of a numerical taxonomy of urban form. *Environment and Planning B*, 49(4), 1283–1299. https://doi.org/10.1177/23998083211059835** [verified]. It uses street network, building footprints and morphological tessellation to derive homogeneous urban tissue types and a hierarchical classification, tested on Prague and Amsterdam. Relevance: the approach can serve as a classifier test. Cluster real tissues (medieval core, Georgian infill, Victorian terraces) and check that generated tissue for era X lands in the right cluster. — [arXiv 2104.14956](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.14956); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/envirb/v49y2022i4p1283-1299.html)
- **Dibble, J., Prelorendjos, A., Romice, O., Zanella, M., Strano, E., Pagel, M., & Porta, S. (2019). On the origin of spaces: Morphometric foundations of urban form evolution. *Environment and Planning B*, 46(4), 707–730. https://doi.org/10.1177/2399808317725075** [verified]. The study measured "Sanctuary Areas" in 45 cities across four periods (Historic/medieval, Industrial, New Towns, Sprawl). Nine dimensions correctly classify about 90% of cases by historic origin, driven mainly by block perimeterness, building coverage and regular plot coverage. Relevance: this is the most direct precedent for an "is this medieval-looking?" classifier. Compute these three variables on generated output and compare them with the Historic-period cluster. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/61294); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/envirb/v46y2019i4p707-730.html)
- **Porta, S., Crucitti, P., & Latora, V. (2006). The network analysis of urban streets: a primal approach. *Environment and Planning B: Planning and Design*, 33(5), 705–725. https://doi.org/10.1068/b32045** [verified]. It analyses intersection-node/street-edge (primal) graphs with metric distance, and it is the basis of Multiple Centrality Assessment (MCA). Relevance: run closeness, betweenness, straightness and information centrality on the generated primal graph. In organic towns, high-centrality corridors should coincide with the market street and the main through-routes. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b32045); [arXiv physics/0506009](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0506009); [PDF](https://webspace.maths.qmul.ac.uk/v.latora/porta_primal_705_epb06.pdf)
- Companion paper [from memory]: Crucitti, P., Latora, V., & Porta, S. (2006). Centrality measures in spatial networks of urban streets. *Physical Review E*, 73, 036125 (doi 10.1103/PhysRevE.73.036125). It compares centrality distributions of self-organised versus planned 1-square-mile samples, and the organic samples included medieval cities (e.g. Venice). Relevance: compare the centrality distribution shapes of generated and real samples.
- **Boeing, G. (2019). Urban spatial order: street network orientation, configuration, and entropy. *Applied Network Science*, 4(1), 67.** [verified citation; DOI expected 10.1007/s41109-019-0189-1, from memory]. It measures street-bearing entropy (weighted and unweighted), mean segment length, circuity, average node degree, and the proportions of four-way intersections and dead-ends across 100 cities using OSM/OSMnx. Chicago, Miami and Minneapolis are the most ordered; Charlotte, São Paulo and Rome the most disordered. Relevance: orientation entropy is a cheap, robust "grid vs organic" check. A medieval English town should show high normalised entropy unless it is a planned bastide or new town (e.g. Edward I's new towns, Salisbury). The metric is easy to implement in JS from polyline bearings. — [author page](https://geoffboeing.com/publications/urban-spatial-order-entropy/); [blog](https://geoffboeing.com/2019/09/urban-street-network-orientation/)
- OSMnx [from memory]: Boeing, G. (2017). OSMnx: New methods for acquiring, constructing, analyzing, and visualizing complex street networks. *Computers, Environment and Urban Systems*, 65, 126–139 (doi 10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2017.05.004). It is the Python tool for downloading OSM street graphs and computing basic stats (intersection density, streets per node, circuity, orientation). Relevance: use it offline to build the reference statistics for real English towns.
- **Louf, R., & Barthélemy, M. (2014). A typology of street patterns. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, 11, 20140924. https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2014.0924** [verified]. A city's "fingerprint" is the conditional distribution of block shape factor (block area divided by the area of its circumscribed circle) given block area. Hierarchical clustering on these fingerprints for 131 cities gives four families. Relevance: compute the area-conditioned shape-factor distribution of generated blocks and compare it to real towns (e.g. with KL or Wasserstein distance). The metric is pure polygon geometry and trivially done in JS. — [arXiv 1410.2094](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.2094)
- **Barthélemy, M., Bordin, P., Berestycki, H., & Gribaudi, M. (2013). Self-organization versus top-down planning in the evolution of a city. *Scientific Reports*, 3, 2153. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep02153** [verified article number; erratum published 12 Nov 2013]. It analyses the Paris street network from 1789 to 2010. Most network measures evolve smoothly. The signatures of Haussmann's central planning are a spatial reorganisation of betweenness centrality and a change in the block-shape distribution. Relevance: this is the template for time-series validation of a growth simulation. Track node, edge and length counts, block shape distribution and the spatial pattern of betweenness over simulated centuries. Expect smooth curves under organic growth and abrupt shifts only for planned interventions. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3703887); [arXiv 1307.2203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.2203)

### Inferences
- A practical JS validation suite could include: (a) orientation entropy (Boeing); (b) block area plus shape-factor fingerprint (Louf & Barthélemy); (c) node-degree distribution, share of dead-ends and 4-way nodes, meshedness, circuity (Boeing; Porta); (d) centrality maps (Porta MCA); (e) building coverage, block perimeter and regular-plot coverage (Dibble). Compare distributions with Wasserstein or KS distance against reference towns processed offline with momepy/OSMnx.
- Barthélemy et al. 2013 is the closest methodological match for a *growth* simulator, because it validates trajectories as well as end states.

### Gaps
- No JavaScript port of momepy or OSMnx was found; metric formulas would need to be reimplemented (turf.js for geometry; graphology-metrics / ngraph.centrality for centrality, see Q3).
- The precise DOIs for the morphological tessellation (CEUS 2020), Boeing 2019 and OSMnx 2017 papers were not confirmed this session.

## Q2. Plot/parcel metrics: frontage and depth, shape, plot series, coverage (Bobkova; Berghauser Pont & Haupt; Conzen)

### Takeaway
Plot-level validation should draw on Conzen's burgage-plot concepts (long narrow plots at right angles to the street, and the burgage cycle of progressive back-plot infill). Quantitative plot attributes are available from Bobkova et al. 2021 (k-means plot typologies). Coverage and density can be described with Spacematrix (GSI/FSI/OSR).

### Cited Findings
- **Conzen, M. R. G. (1960). *Alnwick, Northumberland: A study in town-plan analysis*. Institute of British Geographers Publication 27, London (2nd ed. 1969).** [verified]. This is the founding text of English town-plan analysis. It introduced the burgage cycle, plan units and fringe belts. Relevance: it supplies the qualitative "ground truth" behaviours to test for. Plots should stay persistent over centuries, back-plot coverage should rise and then clear, and fringe belts should form at the edge of each growth phase. An open PDF copy is hosted at FEUP (Vitor Oliveira). — [PDF (FEUP)](https://vitoroliveira.fe.up.pt/alnwick.pdf); [Urban Morphology journal discussion](https://journal.urbanform.org/index.php/jum/article/download/4637/3676/12584)
- **Bobkova, E., Berghauser Pont, M., & Marcus, L. (2021). Towards analytical typologies of plot systems: Quantitative profile of five European cities. *Environment and Planning B*, 48(4), 604–620.** [verified citation; DOI not captured]. It classifies plot systems in five European cities using unsupervised k-means clustering on numeric plot attributes derived from urban-morphology theory. Relevance: this is the closest published set of plot attributes (size, frontage, compactness and similar) to reuse as validation features for generated parcels. — [Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/513739); [full text PDF](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/513739/file/513739_Fulltext.pdf)
- **Berghauser Pont, M., & Haupt, P. (2010). *Spacematrix: Space, Density and Urban Form*. NAi Publishers, Rotterdam.** [book; publisher/year from memory]. It treats density as several variables: FSI (floor space index), GSI (ground space index, i.e. coverage), OSR (open space ratio), L (average number of storeys), plus network density N and block size W. Relevance: GSI per plot, block or tessellation cell is the direct "building coverage" check. Plotting generated and real areas on the GSI–FSI chart shows whether generated tissue sits in the "historic low-rise compact" region. All of these measures are simple area ratios and easy to compute in JS. — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_0b2ef4f4-d21e-42cd-a515-8e3da4b030ae); [cluster-analysis application](https://riunet.upv.es/entities/publication/e8abbd13-e397-45ce-b13a-591a2007cdc7)
- Dibble et al. 2019 (above) identify **building coverage** and **regular plot coverage** among the three variables that best separate historic-period tissue. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/61294)
- Morphological tessellation (Fleischmann et al. 2020, above) is a proxy for plots where cadastral polygons are unavailable. — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/70666)

### Inferences
- Suggested plot checks: the frontage/depth ratio distribution of street-facing plots (burgage plots are long and narrow); plot orientation relative to the street (near-perpendicular); spatial autocorrelation of plot width along a street to detect plot series (runs of similar-width plots from a single laying-out episode); GSI per plot over simulated time (burgage-cycle rise and fall); and the persistence of plot boundaries across time steps.
- Frontage and depth are not standard momepy outputs. They need a custom definition: the shared edge with the street-facing block boundary, and the plot extent perpendicular to it.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published, quantitative "plot-series detection" algorithm. Conzen-school literature treats plot series qualitatively. This needs a design decision or further search (e.g. Kropf; Whitehand; the Urban Morphology journal).
- I found no published numeric distributions of English medieval burgage widths. Values such as one perch (about 5 m) or multiples are sometimes cited in the literature, but none was verified this session.

## Q3. Space syntax measures and tools (Hillier & Iida; depthmapX; angular analysis)

### Takeaway
Angular (least-angle) integration and choice are the standard space syntax measures. Hillier & Iida 2005 show that angular measures predict movement better than metric ones. depthmapX (open source, C++) and sDNA are the reference tools. For in-browser use, approximate with angular-weighted shortest paths on a segment graph using JS graph libraries.

### Cited Findings
- **Hillier, B., & Iida, S. (2005). Network and psychological effects in urban movement. In *Spatial Information Theory: COSIT 2005*, LNCS 3693, pp. 475–490. Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/11556114_30** [verified]. It shows that urban movement correlates better with geometric and topological (least-angle, fewest-turns) network properties than with metric distance, and argues this is a cognitive effect. Relevance: it justifies angular integration (closeness) and angular choice (betweenness) on segment maps as checks. In real market towns the market place and the high street should have the highest angular integration. — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1232/)
- **depthmapX**: originally Depthmap by Alasdair Turner (UCL Space Syntax Lab), redeveloped and open-sourced by Tasos Varoudis in 2012. It is multi-platform with a command-line interface and is available at github.com/SpaceGroupUCL/depthmapX. The QGIS "Space Syntax Toolkit" plug-in provides a front end. Relevance: it is the gold-standard implementation for computing reference values on real towns offline. No browser build was found. — [UCL Bartlett](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/architecture/research/space-syntax/depthmapx)
- **sDNA** (Cardiff University) is another spatial-network-analysis tool covering angular and Euclidean betweenness and closeness. — [About sDNA](https://sdna.cardiff.ac.uk/sdna/about/)
- **JS libraries usable in the browser:** graphology-metrics (betweenness centrality with edge weights and normalisation) — [npm](https://npmjs.com/package/graphology-metrics); ngraph.centrality (Brandes betweenness, closeness) — [README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/ngraph.centrality@2.2.0/README.md); Cytoscape.js (betweenness, closeness, degree). Angular analysis can be emulated by building a segment-dual graph whose edge weights are turn angles, then running weighted closeness and betweenness.

### Inferences
- Run MCA-style primal centralities (Porta) and angular segment centralities (Hillier) on both generated and reference towns, then compare (i) centrality distributions and (ii) whether the top-ranked segments coincide with functionally expected streets (the market place and the routes to gates and bridges).
- Brandes betweenness is O(nm). This is fine in the browser for a market town of a few thousand segments, but radius-limited (local) measures will be needed for larger outputs.

### Gaps
- I found no maintained JS/WebAssembly port of depthmapX. A WASM build of its C++ core looks feasible but is unverified.

## Q4. How procedural/generative city research evaluates realism

### Takeaway
Most procedural-modelling papers rely on visual comparison and user studies, sometimes adding simple statistical comparisons against example cities. Recent deep generative layout papers add distributional distances (Wasserstein over building counts and geometry, overlap and out-of-block indices, FID/KID on rasterised maps). None uses the morphometric literature systematically, which leaves a methodological gap this project could fill.

### Cited Findings
- **Nishida, G., Garcia-Dorado, I., & Aliaga, D. G. (2016). Example-driven procedural urban roads. *Computer Graphics Forum*, 35(6), 5–17. https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.12728** [verified]. It synthesises road networks by combining example-based growth (copying patches from real examples) with procedural growth driven by statistics extracted from the examples, adapting to terrain and existing roads. Relevance: its statistics-driven growth (segment length and curvature distributions extracted from real networks) could serve directly as validation targets. — [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/items/7a226fc0-3502-41ff-a14c-69b95d6e1dd0/full); [project page](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/cgvlab/www/publications/nishida2016example)
- **Aliaga, D. G., Vanegas, C. A., & Beneš, B. (2008). Interactive example-based urban layout synthesis. *ACM Transactions on Graphics* (SIGGRAPH Asia), 27(5).** [verified existence; DOI 10.1145/1409060.1409113 from memory]. It is an example-based synthesis of street and parcel layouts from real city fragments. Relevance: an early precedent for "match the style of a real example". — [SIGGRAPH history](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=195190)
- **Kelly, G., & McCabe, H. (2006). A survey of procedural techniques for city generation. *ITB Journal*, 14.** [verified citation]. It is a widely cited early survey of L-system and other procedural city methods, which were evaluated mainly qualitatively. — [reference via SIGGRAPH history entry for same authors](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=142350)
- **Hartmann, S., Weinmann, M., Wessel, R., & Klein, R. (2017). StreetGAN: Towards road network synthesis with generative adversarial networks. WSCG 2017.** [verified title/venue; author list from memory]. It rasterises road networks, trains a GAN and extracts a graph from the output. Results are evaluated both visually and with statistical road-network similarity measures. — [record](https://dspace.zcu.cz/items/5fb1aa2d-caf0-470c-af2f-7546b9b5beea/full)
- **Chu, H., Li, D., Acuna, D., Kar, A., Shugrina, M., Wei, X., Liu, M.-Y., Torralba, A., & Fidler, S. (2019). Neural Turtle Graphics for modeling city road layouts. ICCV 2019.** [verified title/venue; author list from memory]. It is a sequential generative model of spatial graphs (node and edge at a time) trained on OSM. The authors report outperforming baselines on "a set of diverse performance metrics" and allow style control that mimics existing cities. — [CVF open access](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_ICCV_2019/html/Chu_Neural_Turtle_Graphics_for_Modeling_City_Road_Layouts_ICCV_2019_paper.html); [NVIDIA project](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/toronto-ai/NTG)
- **He, L., & Aliaga, D. (2023). GlobalMapper: Arbitrary-shaped urban layout generation. ICCV 2023, pp. 454–464.** [verified]. It is a graph-attention generator of building layouts within arbitrary blocks, trained on 28 large cities. Evaluation includes a user study plus geometric fidelity (building-shape average Hausdorff distance 1.36 m, IoU 95.78% in the supplement). — [CVF](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2023/html/He_GlobalMapper_Arbitrary-Shaped_Urban_Layout_Generation_ICCV_2023_paper.html); [arXiv 2307.09693](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.09693)
- **He, L., & Aliaga, D. (2024). COHO: Context-sensitive city-scale hierarchical urban layout generation. ECCV 2024.** [verified]. It is a graph masked autoencoder over buildings, blocks and communities across 330 US cities, evaluated on realism, semantic consistency and correctness. Code and data are released at github.com/Arking1995/COHO. Recent layout-generation work in this line uses an overlap index (percentage of overlapping building area), an out-block index (percentage of building area outside block contours), and Wasserstein distances between real and generated distributions of building counts and bounding-box geometry, alongside FID/KID/LPIPS on rendered maps. — [arXiv 2407.11294](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.11294); [ECCV](https://eccv2024.ecva.net/virtual/2024/oral/1610)

### Inferences
- For a vector simulator, the overlap and out-block indices are useful *validity* checks (no building crossing a parcel or street; no overlapping footprints). Wasserstein distance over per-element distributions (block area, shape factor, plot frontage, footprint area and elongation) is a sound, explainable *plausibility* score. FID on rasterised plans is less interpretable but could be added.
- The deep-learning papers almost all target modern US or global cities. Their training and evaluation data is not representative of medieval English tissue, so the reference sets must be built from English historic towns (Q5).

### Gaps
- Full texts of NTG, StreetGAN and Nishida et al. could not be fetched (network errors), so their exact metric lists are not recorded here.
- I did not locate a dedicated survey on *evaluation methodology* for procedural city generators. Recent surveys exist (e.g. a 2026 Springer *Computational Urban Science* paper on procedural generation for city representation, [link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43762-026-00263-8)), but their evaluation content was not checked.

## Q5. Datasets of historical town plans and morphology for comparison

### Takeaway
For English market towns, reference geometry can come from OSM (current streets and footprints, ODbL), historic OS maps (Historic Digimap, georeferenced 1840s–1930s town plans, which need digitising) and the Extensive Urban Survey reports (plan-unit and period mapping). The Edward I new-towns digital atlas provides downloadable GIS data for planned medieval towns. Conzen's Alnwick plans remain the canonical qualitative benchmark.

### Cited Findings
- **Historic Digimap (EDINA)**: Landmark historic Ordnance Survey maps of Great Britain, 1843–1996, including County Series (1843–1939) and Town Plans (1840s–1930s). Over 400,000 georeferenced images can be downloaded for GIS/CAD use (UK academic subscription required). Relevance: the pre-modern-redevelopment plot pattern of market towns can be digitised from the 1:500/1:528 town plans. — [Canterbury Christ Church guide](https://www.canterbury.ac.uk/guides/library/database-guide-historic-digimap); [EDINA help PDF](https://digimap.edina.ac.uk/webhelp/digimapsupport/historic-digimap2019.pdf)
- **Extensive Urban Survey (EUS)**: a national English Heritage/Historic England programme launched in 1992 covering the archaeology, topography and historic buildings of England's historic towns. County datasets on the Archaeology Data Service each have a DOI, e.g. Wiltshire (34 towns), Derbyshire (22 small towns), Gloucestershire (37 settlements), Cheshire (37 towns, 73 reports), Cambridgeshire (2017). Relevance: plan-unit and period-of-origin maps give per-town "which parts are medieval" masks, so metrics are computed only on medieval tissue. Many are PDF-only, and some include GIS. — [ADS Wiltshire](https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archives/view/wiltshire_eus_2009/overview.cfm); [ADS Cheshire](https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archives/view/cheshire_eus_2013/overview.cfm); [ADS Cambridgeshire](https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archives/view/cambrs_eus_2017/overview.cfm); [Staffordshire EUS](https://www.staffordshire.gov.uk/environment/countryside/historic-environment/extensive-urban-survey-project)
- **Mapping the Medieval Townscape: a digital atlas of the new towns of Edward I** (ADS, AHRB 2005). It offers interactive maps with historical, architectural, archaeological and survey data, plus downloadable raw data. Relevance: these are reference cases for *planned* medieval towns (grid-like, low orientation entropy), as distinct from organic market towns. — [ADS](https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archives/view/atlas_ahrb_2005/atlas.cfm)
- **British Historic Towns Atlas** (Historic Towns Trust): its volumes (e.g. York, Cambridge) include researched reconstruction maps, some available as PDFs. Relevance: historical reconstructions of medieval street and plot layout at known dates support time-series validation. — [Cambridge library online maps](https://www.lib.cam.ac.uk/node/18742)
- **Atlas of Rural Settlement in England** (Roberts & Wrathmell) is available as an ArcGIS feature service. It gives regional settlement-type context rather than plot geometry. — [ArcGIS service](https://services6.arcgis.com/LBG1Lnci7jBl9O0t/ArcGIS/rest/services/Atlas_of_Rural_Settlement/FeatureServer)
- **OpenStreetMap via OSMnx**: Boeing 2019 used OSM street networks for 100 cities, so the same pipeline extends directly to English market towns. — [Boeing 2019](https://geoffboeing.com/publications/urban-spatial-order-entropy/)
- **Conzen's Alnwick** plan analysis (open PDF): [FEUP](https://vitoroliveira.fe.up.pt/alnwick.pdf)

### Inferences
- A suggested reference-set pipeline: pick about 10–20 English market towns with well-preserved medieval cores (Alnwick, Ludlow, Stratford, Thame, etc.). Take OSM streets and footprints (or OS OpenMap Local / VectorMap), mask them to EUS-mapped medieval plan units, generate morphological tessellation as a plot proxy, and compute momepy + OSMnx metrics offline. Export the distributions as JSON for the browser to compare against.
- Modern OSM footprints include post-medieval rebuilding. Digitising 19th-century OS Town Plans gives a closer pre-industrial baseline but costs manual effort.

### Gaps
- I did not verify whether Historic England publishes vector GIS of EUS plan units nationally. Availability appears to vary by county.
- OS MasterMap Topography (cadastral-quality footprints) licensing was not checked. OS OpenMap Local building footprints (open) were not verified this session.
- I found no ready-made open dataset of digitised medieval English burgage plots.
