# Trail, Path and Route-Network Emergence: Annotated Bibliography for a Movement-Driven Town Growth Simulation

Scope note: about 15 web searches and fetches. Items marked **[V]** had their citation or venue confirmed by a search result this session. Items marked **[M]** are cited from memory: they are well known, but their DOI or pages were not re-checked this session and should be verified before publication. The already-known core works (Helbing et al 1997 Nature; Barthélemy & Flammini 2008; Strano et al 2012; Emilien et al 2012) are not re-annotated except where a follow-up is needed.

## 1. Follow-ups and extensions of active walker / trail formation models (and vector vs raster output)

### Takeaway
The active walker family (Helbing; Goldstone) and Physarum or ant-style reinforcement models all keep their state on a **raster (ground or chemoattractant field)**. The one exception is the Tero et al Physarum model, which works on a **predefined graph** and adapts edge conductances. Our hybrid design matches this split: a raster for wear, plus a vector graph whose edge weights are reinforced the Tero way. No trail model found produces a vector street graph directly.

### Cited Findings
- **Helbing, D., Schweitzer, F., Keltsch, J., Molnár, P. (1997). "Active walker model for the formation of human and animal trail systems." *Phys. Rev. E* 56, 2527.** DOI [10.1103/PhysRevE.56.2527](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevE.56.2527); open preprint [arXiv cond-mat/9806097](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/9806097) **[V]**. This is the full-length companion to the Nature letter. Each walker obeys an equation of motion, an equation for environmental change (the ground "comfort" field, which builds up with footprints and decays over time), and an orientation relation that blends the direction to the destination with the gradient of the trail field. It reproduces trail bundling and "minimal detour" systems. *Borrow:* the explicit ground-potential PDE (growth from walking, exponential weathering decay) and the visibility-weighted orientation kernel, as the core of our wear raster. *Gap:* output is a continuous field, so trails must be extracted. Walker rules are not tied to terrain cost. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9806097)
- **Helbing, D. et al. (1998). "Computer simulations of pedestrian dynamics and trail formation."** [arXiv cond-mat/9805074](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9805074) **[V]**. Couples the social-force pedestrian model with footprint trails that decay exponentially. Pedestrians prefer frequently used routes and so generate minimal detour systems. *Borrow:* evidence that trail formation drops straight into an agent movement model. Its decay-constant and trail-attractiveness parameters set the balance between "direct paths" and "bundled trunk paths". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/9805074)
- **Goldstone, R. L., Jones, A., Roberts, M. E. (2006). "Group path formation." *IEEE Trans. Systems, Man, and Cybernetics – Part A* 36(3), 611–620.** **[V venue]**. A lab experiment with real people moving between destinations in a virtual world, where the cost of stepping on a cell falls the more often others have visited it. It compares observed human paths with an active-walker model. *Borrow:* empirical calibration of how strongly reinforcement should beat directness. Also shows trails form even when agents are purely goal-driven and non-cooperative. *Gap:* small grid world, no terrain. Lab page: [Indiana PCL "Group path formation"](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/?p=404). A related paper, **Goldstone & Roberts (2006) "Self-organized trail systems in groups of humans," *Complexity* 11(6), 43–50 [M; DOI likely 10.1002/cplx.20135]**, is cited in the literature (see [arXiv 1005.3507](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1005.3507)), but I did not open the primary record.
- **Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., Yumiki, K., Kobayashi, R., Nakagaki, T. (2010). "Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design." *Science* 327(5964), 439–442.** DOI [10.1126/science.1177894](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1177894) **[M]**. Physarum was grown on a map of the Tokyo-area cities and formed networks comparable to the rail system in cost, efficiency and fault tolerance. The paper gives a mathematical model with **flux-driven conductance reinforcement on a graph**, where edge conductance grows with the flow through it and decays otherwise. *Borrow:* this is the cleanest vector-native reinforcement rule. Apply it to our street-graph edges: conductance becomes the "wear/usage" score, and thresholds on it drive the track → lane → street upgrade. *Gap:* it needs a dense candidate graph up front, and its source/sink flows are static.
- **Tero, A., Kobayashi, R., Nakagaki, T. (2007). "A mathematical model for adaptive transport network in path finding by true slime mold." *J. Theor. Biol.* 244(4), 553–564.** DOI [10.1016/j.jtbi.2006.07.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2006.07.015) **[M]**. The original form of the conductance-adaptation ODE used in the 2010 paper. *Borrow:* the update rule dD/dt = f(|Q|) − γD and how the choice of f (sigmoidal vs linear) shifts the outcome between a tree and a redundant loopy network.
- **Jones, J. (2010). "Characteristics of pattern formation and evolution in approximations of Physarum transport networks." *Artificial Life* 16(2), 127–153.** **[M; DOI likely 10.1162/artl.2010.16.2.16202]**. Agents sense and deposit chemoattractant on a raster, and they collectively form and shrink transport networks that resemble proximity graphs. Repository listing: [UWE Jeff Jones outputs](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/980579) **[V author/topic]**. *Borrow:* a GPU-friendly sense-deposit-diffuse-decay loop, a good fit for a browser (WebGL) wear raster. *Gap:* raster only, with no geometry extraction.
- **Adamatzky, A. & Jones, J. (2010). "Road planning with slime mould: if Physarum built motorways it would route M6/M74 through Newcastle." *Int. J. Bifurcation and Chaos* 20(10), 3065–3084.** **[M; DOI likely 10.1142/S0218127410027568]**. Slime mould grown on a UK map with oat flakes at the major urban areas, compared against the motorway network. *Borrow:* a UK-scale validation idea. Settlements act as fixed nutrient sources, which is like our attractors.
- **Kwak et al.** (requested): no trail-formation paper by a "Kwak" was found in my searches. See Gaps.

### Inferences
- A practical hybrid: (a) a Helbing or Jones-style **wear raster** to find where paths form, which is cheap and fits WebGL; (b) periodic extraction to a vector graph (Section 5); (c) **Tero conductance dynamics** on that graph's edges for long-term hierarchy and upgrades. The raster settles geometry and the graph settles hierarchy.
- In Helbing's model the trail decay rate is the key parameter between "every desire line" and "a few trunk routes". Over centuries, decay should be slow for streets with buildings and fast for field tracks.

### Gaps
- I could not identify the "Kwak et al" trail model the brief names. It may be confused with another author, or with a Korean pedestrian-dynamics group. It needs the user's exact reference.
- I found no ant-colony-optimisation (ACO) paper aimed at *emergent road geometry*, as opposed to routing on a fixed graph. Classic ACO (Dorigo) is graph-based and works like a stochastic Tero model.

## 2. Archaeological least-cost path (LCP) and route network models (incl. Tobler, holloways)

### Takeaway
Archaeological practice has moved from single LCPs to **cumulative, "everywhere to everywhere" corridor and network models**. Methodological critiques stress anisotropic slope costs, algorithm or neighbourhood artefacts, and the lack of validation. White & Barber's FETE is the closest analogue to our walker-density raster. Holloway formation is studied in geomorphology as traffic-plus-water gully erosion, with measured incision rates usable for a "terrain deepening" feedback.

### Cited Findings
- **White, D. A. & Barber, S. B. (2012). "Geospatial modeling of pedestrian transportation networks: a case study from precolumbian Oaxaca, Mexico." *J. Archaeol. Sci.* 39(8), 2684–2696.** **[V title/journal/year; DOI likely 10.1016/j.jas.2012.04.017, M]**. The "From Everywhere to Everywhere" (FETE) model builds natural-looking networks from topography and land cover **without pre-specified origins and destinations**, and estimates traffic intensity per segment by summing many LCPs. It was validated against known late precolumbian and early colonial corridors. Open PDF: [colorado.edu](https://www.colorado.edu/rioverdearchaeology/sites/default/files/attached-files/white_barber_2012.pdf); record: [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib2010/3484). *Borrow:* use summed-LCP traffic as a **fast bootstrap** for the initial regional track network before walkers or buildings exist. Its per-segment traffic counts map directly onto our upgrade thresholds. *Gap:* static. There is no reinforcement, so worn paths do not lower future costs.
- **Herzog, I. (2014). "Least-cost paths – some methodological issues." *Internet Archaeology* 36.** DOI 10.11141/ia.36.5 **[V title/issue; DOI M]**. Open: [intarch.ac.uk](https://intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue36/5/index.html). Reviews LCP algorithms, raster neighbourhood size (4/8/16/48 connectivity and its distortions), **anisotropic vs isotropic slope costs**, the properties of least-cost distances, and cost components: slope, vegetation, wetlands, river crossings and fords, topography, and social or cultural costs. Warns against default GIS settings. *Borrow:* a checklist for our terrain cost raster. Use 16+ neighbourhoods or any-angle pathfinding (for example Theta*) to avoid zig-zag artefacts. Treat river crossings as point costs at fords. Make slope cost direction-dependent. Herzog also has related work on **least-cost networks**: [CAA2012 slides "Least cost networks"](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~ucakche/agdank/agdankht2012/CAA2012_Least_cost_networks_IHerzog.pdf) **[V]**.
- **Verhagen, P., Nuninger, L., Groenhuijzen, M. R. (2019). "Modelling of pathways and movement networks in archaeology: an overview of current approaches." In Verhagen, Joyce & Groenhuijzen (eds), *Finding the Limits of the Limes*, Springer, Computational Social Sciences, ch. 11.** DOI [10.1007/978-3-030-04576-0_11](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-04576-0_11) **[V]** (open access; [VU record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/modelling-of-pathways-and-movement-networks-in-archaeology-an-ove/)). The state of the art in LCP, network reconstruction and network analysis for ancient movement in the Roman frontier zone. Concludes the field needs integrated approaches, sensitivity analysis and validation, and stronger theory. *Borrow:* the review's taxonomy (LCP → cumulative LCP → network reconstruction, for example proximity graphs and gravity models) is a good framing for our design doc. *Gap identified there:* few models couple path formation dynamically with settlement, which is our niche.
- **Tobler, W. (1993). "Three presentations on geographical analysis and modeling." NCGIA Technical Report 93-1.** **[M]**. Source of Tobler's hiking function, *v = 6·exp(−3.5·|tan θ + 0.05|)* km/h. It is anisotropic, with the fastest speed on a slight downhill (about −2.86°). *Borrow:* the default slope-to-speed function for walkers. Herzog (2014, above) discusses its limits and alternatives, such as quadratic or "wheeled vehicle" cost functions for carts on lanes and streets.
- **Llobera, M., Fábrega-Álvarez, P., Parcero-Oubiña, C. (2011). "Order in movement: a GIS approach to accessibility." *J. Archaeol. Sci.* 38(4), 843–851.** **[M; DOI likely 10.1016/j.jas.2010.11.006]**. Proposes "focal mobility networks" and accessibility measures built from many LCPs, as opposed to single routes. *Borrow:* an accessibility surface could serve as the land-value or attractiveness field for where buildings get placed.
- **Holloways and sunken lanes (geomorphology).** KU Leuven research on sunken lanes in the European loess belt treats them as gullies initiated and deepened by **traffic erosion plus concentrated runoff**. One paper tests whether the gully slope-area threshold *S = kA^b* predicts lane-head initiation, using LiDAR and field data on 132 lane heads in the Belgian loess belt: "Does the topographic threshold concept explain the initiation points of sunken lanes in the European loess belt?" [KU Leuven Lirias PDF](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/574468) **[V title; authors not confirmed]**. Reported medium- to long-term incision rates for unpaved, bare sunken lanes are **1–5 cm/yr**, at least an order of magnitude above nearby cropland erosion. Summarised in [Wikipedia "Sunken lane"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunken_lane), which cites the Leuven work; secondary source. Related record: [Lirias 653832](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/handle/123456789/653832). *Borrow:* a terrain feedback where heavily used, unpaved track segments on slopes lower the DEM by about 1–5 cm/yr. Over 3–5 centuries that gives 3–25 m-deep holloways, a recognisable medieval English feature. Deepened lanes then channel runoff and become harder to leave, which adds lock-in.

### Inferences
- Pipeline suggestion: Tobler-based anisotropic cost → FETE-like summed LCP for the initial regional track skeleton → walker reinforcement takes over locally. Fords appear as natural cost minima on river cells.
- Holloway incision gives a physical path-dependence mechanism alongside the "comfort" reinforcement. It would also make the simulation visibly English-medieval.

### Gaps
- I did not verify specific English holloway dating or formation studies (Dorset/Devon) in the peer-reviewed literature. The 1–5 cm/yr figure comes from Belgian loess, and rates on other substrates will differ.
- I did not locate a peer-reviewed model specific to *medieval English* route-network emergence. Roman road LCP studies exist (for example in the Verhagen et al. volume), but I did not annotate them individually.

## 3. Road network growth with feedback between roads and settlement

### Takeaway
Network-growth models fall into economic/investment types (Levinson & Yerra: hierarchy emerges from link revenue and investment), geometric/morphogenetic types (Courtat; Rui et al; Barthélemy & Flammini), and coupled density–network types (Raimbault). Raimbault's model is the closest to our "buildings become attractors" loop, and Levinson & Yerra's is the closest to "heavily used edges upgrade".

### Cited Findings
- **Levinson, D. & Yerra, B. (2006). "Self-organization of surface transportation networks." *Transportation Science* 40(2), 179–188.** DOI [10.1287/trsc.1050.0132](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/trsc.1050.0132) **[V]**; open: [UMN Conservancy](https://conservancy.umn.edu/items/5b109b6a-32b9-4cdb-91f1-918bae5ba9da). A travel-demand model on a grid is coupled to revenue, cost and investment models. Links that earn more than their upkeep get upgraded, and others decay. **Roads (contiguous high-capacity link chains) and hierarchies emerge** from a uniform network, driven by scale (dis)economies, boundaries and initial asymmetry. *Borrow:* the upgrade/downgrade rule. Treat "track → lane → street" as investment in an edge in proportion to its traffic, with maintenance cost causing decay, so the hierarchy emerges and can also be lost. *Gap:* fixed grid topology, so no new links are created.
- **Yerra, B. M. & Levinson, D. M. (2005). "The emergence of hierarchy in transportation networks." *Annals of Regional Science* 39, 541–553.** DOI [10.1007/s00168-005-0230-4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00168-005-0230-4) **[V]**. The companion paper that shows hierarchy emerging from the same link-investment feedback.
- **Xie, F. & Levinson, D. (2009). "Modeling the growth of transportation networks: a comprehensive review." *Networks and Spatial Economics* 9, 291–307.** **[M; DOI likely 10.1007/s11067-007-9037-4]**. Classifies network growth models across transport geography, network science, economics and planning: "spontaneous" vs optimised growth, and topological vs geometric. *Borrow:* a literature map for positioning our model.
- **Courtat, T., Gloaguen, C., Douady, S. (2011). "Mathematics and morphogenesis of cities: a geometrical approach." *Phys. Rev. E* 83, 036106.** Preprint [arXiv 1010.1762](https://arxiv.org/abs/1010.1762) **[V preprint; PRE ref M]**. Introduces a **street-based** (not segment-based) representation: segments are merged into "streets" by continuity of angle at intersections. Static analysis of French towns suggests growth by **division/extension of space**, and a dynamic model with few parameters generates diverse city forms. *Borrow:* (1) the angle-continuity street-merging rule, useful for collapsing our walker edges into named "streets"; (2) a "division" step that subdivides large blocks with new lanes as density rises. *Gap:* geometric rules only, with no walkers or terrain.
- **Rui, Y., Ban, Y., Wang, J., Haas, J. (2013). "Exploring the patterns and evolution of self-organized urban street networks through modeling." *Eur. Phys. J. B* 86(3), 74.** DOI 10.1140/epjb/e2012-30235-7 **[V via IDEAS handle]**; [EPJ B page](https://epjb.epj.org/articles/epjb/abs/2013/03/b120235/b120235.html). A growth model with competing new centres, each with its own attraction radius, and roads added by a local optimum over geometric and topological factors. It reproduces diverse self-organised street patterns, and its statistics match empirical data. *Borrow:* attraction radius per attractor as a parameter (ford vs market vs church). Its local-optimal connection rule is an alternative to Barthélemy-Flammini-style growth.
- **Raimbault, J. "An urban morphogenesis model capturing interactions between networks and territories."** In D'Acci, L. (ed.) *The Mathematics of Urban Morphology* (Birkhäuser, 2019); preprint [arXiv 1805.05195](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10073881/1/1805.05195v2.pdf) **[V title/preprint; book details M]**; record [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10073881/). A stochastic model coupling grid-cell **population density** with a **growing road network**. New population attaches preferentially by a potential combining local density, distance to the network, network centralities and generalised accessibility. The network grows in response. Calibrated on European territorial statistics. *Borrow:* the attachment-potential formulation is almost exactly our "where do buildings go" rule (proximity to streets × centrality × density). Network betweenness feeding back into land attractiveness is a principled way to grow a market or core. Related: [Raimbault, "Modeling the co-evolution of urban form and transportation networks" (CCS 2017)](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-01590624) **[V]**.
- **Barthélemy, M. (2011). "Spatial networks." *Physics Reports* 499(1–3), 1–101.** **[M; DOI likely 10.1016/j.physrep.2010.11.002]**; preprint arXiv:1010.0302 [M]. The standard review of planar and spatial network measures and growth models (optimal networks, greedy growth, local optimisation). *Borrow:* metrics for validating our output graph: degree distribution, meshedness, block-area distribution, route straightness.

### Inferences
- Combine Raimbault (land attachment) + Levinson/Yerra (edge investment and decay) + Tero (flux-driven conductance) into one loop: walkers create flux → flux upgrades edges → edge centrality raises adjacent land attractiveness → buildings appear → buildings become new walker origins and destinations.
- Courtat's street-merging gives a cheap post-process that turns the raw graph into human-readable streets (useful for naming, rendering widths, and frontage).

### Gaps
- None of these models simulates centuries-scale dynamics with a pre-industrial movement mode (foot, horse, cart) or terrain. Raimbault uses abstract grids, and Levinson uses a uniform grid.

## 4. Informal settlement / unplanned path growth (favela and squatter models)

### Takeaway
The informal-settlement modelling literature (Barros, Sobreira) is mostly **cellular-automata (CA) or agent-on-grid** work on where settlement occupies land. Access paths appear as leftover gaps, not modelled entities. It is useful for building-placement rules and fractal-fragmentation checks, but gives little on path geometry.

### Cited Findings
- **Barros, J. & Sobreira, F. (2002). "City of slums: self-organisation across scales." CASA Working Paper 55, UCL.** Open: [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/243/) / [PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/243/1/Paper55.pdf) **[V]**; also at [Birkbeck eprints](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/16470). Treats spontaneous settlements as complex subsystems within the city. It combines a CA/agent model of peripheral informal growth (Barros) with fractal and morphological fragmentation analysis of individual slums (Sobreira). *Borrow:* Sobreira's size-distribution and fragmentation metrics can validate organically grown medieval-like quarters. Barros' "peripherisation" dynamics loosely match suburbs growing outside town gates. *Gap:* access paths are not explicit, and there is no movement-wear mechanism.
- **Sobreira, F. & Gomes, M. "The geometry of slums: boundaries, packing and diversity."** CASA Working Paper 30 (2001) **[M]**. Fractal and packing analysis of favela building footprints. Same relevance as above.

### Inferences
- For path emergence within dense unplanned fabric, the walker-wear model probably beats CA approaches. Lanes there are "what's left between buildings that still gets walked". Our simulation could enforce this by blocking building placement on cells whose wear is above a threshold. That reproduces the pattern where paths are preserved and buildings infill around them.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed favela model where internal access paths emerge from movement. Not searched further: Hillier's "beady ring" (Hillier & Hanson 1984, *The Social Logic of Space*), which is the classic generative model of unplanned settlements around open-space access, and Schaur (1991) *IL 39 Non-planned settlements* (Frei Otto's institute), which documents path-system formation. Both are strong candidates to follow up.

## 5. Practical algorithms: trail raster → vector graph; path merging / bundling

### Takeaway
The most transferable body of work is **map inference from GPS traces**, which solves the same problem as ours: turning many noisy trajectories into a road graph. The kernel-density → threshold → skeletonise → graph-extract → prune pipeline (Biagioni & Eriksson) suits a wear raster directly. Trajectory-clustering and graph-spanner methods (Kharita) suit working from walker polylines. Force-directed edge bundling is a geometry-level way to merge near-parallel paths.

### Cited Findings
- **Biagioni, J. & Eriksson, J. (2012). "Map inference in the face of noise and disparity." *Proc. ACM SIGSPATIAL GIS 2012*.** DOI [10.1145/2424321.2424333](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F2424321.2424333) **[V]**; code: [GitHub skeleton_gis2012](https://github.com/haoranshigrab/skeleton_gis2012) **[V]**. Pipeline: KDE density raster of trips → grayscale skeleton at multiple thresholds → graph extraction → map-matching trips back onto the graph → pruning and refinement. *Borrow:* almost exactly our wear-raster → street-graph step. Multi-threshold skeletonisation keeps faint but real tracks while dropping noise. Map-matching the walkers back onto the graph gives per-edge traffic counts.
- **Ahmed, M., Karagiorgou, S., Pfoser, D., Wenk, C. (2015). "A comparison and evaluation of map construction algorithms using vehicle tracking data." *GeoInformatica* 19, 601–632.** Preprint [arXiv 1402.5138](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1402.5138) **[V preprint; journal ref M]**. Benchmarks the three algorithm families (point clustering/KDE, incremental track insertion, intersection linking) and their quality metrics. *Borrow:* choose the family that fits. Incremental track insertion merges each new walker polyline into the existing graph within a distance and heading tolerance. That suits incremental browser simulation better than periodic re-skeletonisation, because it keeps graph identity stable over time.
- **Stanojevic, R., Abbar, S., Thirumuruganathan, S., Chawla, S., Filali, F., Aleimat, A. (2018). "Robust road map inference through network alignment of trajectories" (Kharita).** Preprint [arXiv 1702.06025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.06025) **[V preprint; venue M, SIAM SDM 2018]**. Clusters trajectory points by position and heading, then builds a sparse graph with a graph spanner. Fast and online. *Borrow:* an online, heading-aware clustering of walker samples into nodes and edges, as an alternative to raster skeletonisation.
- **Holten, D. & van Wijk, J. J. (2009). "Force-directed edge bundling for graph visualization." *Computer Graphics Forum* 28(3), 983–990.** **[M; DOI likely 10.1111/j.1467-8659.2009.01450.x]**. Edges are modelled as springs with subdivision points that attract compatible (near-parallel, nearby) edges, merging them into bundles. *Borrow:* a geometry-only path-merging rule for vector walker paths. Treat "bundling" as the vector analogue of trail attraction, then collapse bundles below a width tolerance into one edge.
- **Galin, E., Peytavie, A., Guérin, E., Beneš, B. (2011). "Authoring hierarchical road networks." *Computer Graphics Forum* 30(7), 2021–2030** **[M]**, and **Galin et al. (2010). "Procedural generation of roads." *CGF* 29(2)** **[M]**. Anisotropic shortest paths over terrain cost (slope, curvature, rivers and bridges) on a generalised k-neighbourhood grid, plus hierarchical road-network construction that merges and reuses existing roads. *Borrow:* the grid "mask" neighbourhood for smooth, low-curvature paths and cost terms for bridges and fords. Their hierarchical merge step is a useful reference for snapping new paths onto existing streets. (Relates to the already-known Emilien et al 2012.)

### Inferences
- Recommended implementation: run walkers on the raster with any-angle or k-neighbourhood pathfinding over cost = terrain cost × (1 − wear bonus). Record each trip polyline. Insert polylines incrementally into the graph (Ahmed et al.'s "incremental track insertion" family), with snapping tolerance scaled by edge class. Every N years, re-skeletonise the wear raster (Biagioni-style) to find new desire lines and to retire unused edges. Use Courtat angle-continuity to group edges into streets.
- Zhang–Suen thinning plus a pixel-graph tracer (as in libraries like `sknw`) and Douglas–Peucker simplification is the standard low-tech raster-to-vector route. These are classical image-processing algorithms and were not separately cited.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published system that runs trail reinforcement and incremental vector-graph construction *in one loop*. This appears to be a genuine novelty of the proposed design.
- Exact citations for thinning algorithms (Zhang & Suen 1984, *CACM* 27(3)) were not verified this session.
