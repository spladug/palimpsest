# Parcel subdivision algorithms and parcel evolution over time: annotated bibliography

Scope note: These notes skip the four works already known (Vanegas et al 2012 parcels; Weber et al 2009; Parish & Müller 2001; Emilien et al 2012). Where they come up, it is only to point to follow-ups. Every item has a link that turned up during research. Several publisher pages (Springer, Chalmers, Glasgow, UOW) refused connections from this environment, so a few details come from search-result abstracts only. Those items are flagged. DOIs I recalled but did not confirm in this session are marked "(DOI unverified)".

## 1. Geometric and procedural parcel subdivision algorithms (beyond Vanegas 2012)

### Takeaway
The procedural literature offers three families of cut that fit the planned split operators: recursive OBB/bisection, straight-skeleton strips that always reach the street, and offset-from-frontage. The GIS line (Dahal & Chow) adds what graphics work tends to skip: access, road continuity and minimum lot constraints. Example-based and inverse methods (Aliaga 2008; Vanegas 2012 ToG; Zhang 2020) show how to fit split parameters to real plot patterns. Deep generative work is still mostly raster or graph based and does not model plot history.

### Cited Findings
- **CityEngine block/lot subdivision docs (technical reference).** Esri, *ArcGIS CityEngine Help: Block Parameters* and *Subdivide Shapes settings (Python API)*. Three modes. "Recursive Subdivision creates rectangular lots by repeatedly splitting the block." "Offset Subdivision creates lots only within a given distance from the street edges." "Skeleton Subdivision creates street-aligned lots that always have access." **Relevance:** the industry baseline for the three operator types. Skeleton mode maps onto lengthwise burgage cuts (strips perpendicular to the frontage) and recursive mode onto depthwise or back-land splits. — [CityEngine Block Parameters](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/2019.0/help/help-layers-block-parameters.htm); [Subdivide shapes settings](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/latest/python/subdivide-shapes-settings.htm)
- **Kelly, T. (2013). *Unwritten procedural modeling with the straight skeleton*. PhD thesis, University of Glasgow.** Develops generalisations of the straight skeleton, including the "mixed weighted straight skeleton", for urban procedural modelling, and applies the skeleton to subdividing city blocks into parcels. Kelly co-authored Vanegas 2012, so this is the skeleton technique behind that paper. **Relevance:** a weighted skeleton gives per-edge control (frontage weight vs side/back weight), which fits the tagged-edge plots well. Gap: no temporal model. — [Glasgow theses 4975](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/4975) (the page did not load here; details come from the search abstract)
- **Dahal, K. R. & Chow, T. E. (2014). "A GIS toolset for automated partitioning of urban lands." *Environmental Modelling & Software* 55, 222–234.** Related conference version: "Parcel subdivision automation for agent-based land use modelling" (UOW). The "Parcel-Divider" GIS toolset automates neighbourhood subdivision inside an agent-based land-use model. It "generates uniform and regularly-shaped lots while maintaining egress and continuity of road networks to the newly developed areas." **Relevance:** the clearest precedent for running subdivision *inside* a growth simulation, with new access roads cut into large parcels. Borrow its access and road-continuity constraints for back-land development, and later for 19th-century railroad-town gridding. — [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/articles/conference_contribution/Parcel_subdivision_automation_for_agent-based_land_use_modelling/27694788); citation confirmed via [Temple profile](https://liberalarts.temple.edu/directory/khila-dahal)
- **Dahal, K. R. (thesis). *Urban Growth Simulation Through Agent-Integrated Irregular Automata (AIIA)*. Texas State University.** Pairs irregular (parcel-shaped) cellular automata with agents, so the simulation cells are real parcels rather than a grid. **Relevance:** a model in which parcels are the dynamic unit, as in the planned simulation. — [Texas State repository](https://digital.library.txst.edu/items/6836ba52-e695-48d4-96de-9415469dfd6d/full)
- **Aliaga, D. G., Vanegas, C. A. & Beneš, B. (2008). "Interactive example-based urban layout synthesis." *ACM TOG* 27(5) (SIGGRAPH Asia).** Synthesises street networks plus parcels and aerial imagery from example fragments of real cities, "ranging from hundreds to thousands of city blocks and parcels." **Relevance:** an example-based way to learn plot-shape statistics (width/depth distributions per style) from historic maps. — [PDF](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Aliaga08ToG.pdf); [DOI 10.1145/1409060.1409113](https://doi.org/10.1145/1409060.1409113)
- **Vanegas, C. A., Garcia-Dorado, I., Aliaga, D. G., Beneš, B. & Waddell, P. (2012). "Inverse design of urban procedural models." *ACM TOG* 31(6).** Lets users control a procedural city either forward (parameters) or inverse (by setting target indicator values). It is a follow-up to the known Vanegas EG 2012 parcels paper and couples it to UrbanSim. **Relevance:** a route to calibrating split parameters against target metrics such as mean frontage width and plot density. — [PDF](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Vanegas12ToG.pdf)
- **Zhang, X., et al. (2020). "Automatic deep inference of procedural cities from global-scale spatial data." *ACM Trans. Spatial Algorithms and Systems*.** An inverse procedural model that splits a city into blocks and uses deep networks to classify blocks and estimate their procedural (parcel/building) parameters. **Relevance:** learns parameters, not geometry, so its outputs could drive a hand-written split engine. — [PDF](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/bbenes/papers/Zhang20TSAS.pdf); [Crossref 10.1145/3423422](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3423422)
- **ML/generative layouts (survey-level).** Recent work conditions Pix2Pix/GAN variants (ESGAN, CAIN-GAN) and diffusion/ControlNet models on road and parcel inputs. A 2025 framework generates large-scale *vector* 3D urban layouts as graphs. Most such models are image based and "do not explicitly consider topological relationships." **Relevance:** mostly a gap. None model plot lineage, so they are useful at most for producing initial layouts. — [arXiv 2509.23804](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.23804); [arXiv 2409.17049](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.17049)

### Inferences
- Skeleton subdivision is the natural fit for "lengthwise split with direction interpolated between side boundaries". The skeleton's medial structure already interpolates between the bounding edges. A per-edge weight could encode how strongly old ghost boundaries attract new cuts.
- None of the graphics papers found implement *ghost-boundary preference* or a split/merge history. That part of the design looks new relative to this prior art.

### Gaps
- **"Wickramasinghe et al parcel generation"**: two searches found no such paper. It may be misremembered, or may be a minor conference paper. Do not cite it without a source.
- I could not fetch the full text of Dahal & Chow 2014 (connection refused) to confirm its geometric algorithm. Its DOI is not verified here.

## 2. Models of parcel dynamics over time (ABMs, land markets, cadastral change)

### Takeaway
Parcel-level ABMs (UrbanSim, CHALMS, the Filatova/Parker land market) model *who develops which parcel and when*, driven by prices and agent types. Most hold parcel geometry fixed, or allow only coarse conversion such as farm to subdivision. Cadastral database research supplies a clean event vocabulary for geometry change (split, union, reallocation, boundary modification) and topology checks. That vocabulary matches the planned split/merge operators almost one to one.

### Cited Findings
- **Waddell, P. (2002). "UrbanSim: Modeling urban development for land use, transportation, and environmental planning." *JAPA* 68(3), 297–314** (DOI 10.1080/01944360208976274, unverified), plus the parcel-level extension (Waddell et al., *J. Transport and Land Use*, San Francisco prototype). UrbanSim "uses individual land ownership parcels as the basic geographic unit for real estate development." Its parcel-level developer model uses "a development template approach" to represent heterogeneous real estate. **Relevance:** borrow the development-template idea (building types allowed per parcel shape) and its event loop. Gap: parcel geometry is mostly fixed input. — [UrbanSim JAPA PDF](https://nature.berkeley.edu/~penggong/PDFpapers/UrbanSim-JAPA.pdf); [JTLU parcel prototype](https://jtlu.org/index.php/jtlu/article/view/124)
- **Magliocca, N., Safirova, E., McConnell, V. & Walls, M. (2011). "An economic agent-based model of coupled housing and land markets (CHALMS)." *Computers, Environment and Urban Systems* 35(3).** Consumer, farmer and developer agents convert farmland to housing in a growing exurban area. Developers choose lot sizes and densities from price expectations, so "the model can represent the effects of land and housing prices on housing density over time." Code is on CoMSES. **Relevance:** the closest economic model for *when* and *how finely* a farm parcel gets subdivided. Useful for a railroad-town land-sale phase. — [CoMSES model 3368](https://www.comses.net/codebases/3368); [publication record](https://catalog.comses.net/publications/2419)
- **Filatova, T., Parker, D. C. & van der Veen, A. (2008). "A conceptual design for a bilateral agent-based land market with heterogeneous economic agents." *CEUS* 32(6), 454–463**, and **Filatova, Parker & van der Veen (2009). "Agent-based urban land markets: agent's pricing behavior, land prices and urban land use change." *JASSS* 12(1) 3.** Bilateral trades between households, developers and rural landowners, with bid/ask price formation and price expectations based on local neighbourhoods. **Relevance:** a market rule for when adjacent plots change hands, which is needed before same-owner merges can happen. — [MSU summary of 2008 paper](https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/conceptual-design-bilateral-agent-based-land-market-heterogeneous-economic-agents); [JASSS 12(1)3](https://jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk/12/1/3.html)
- **Residential developer ABM (Auckland).** A spatial multi-agent model of how developers "purchase and subdivide land." Developer behaviour depends on available capital, including how they read markets, rank parcels and time subdivision. **Relevance:** developer heterogeneity drives subdivision timing. — [ResearchSpace Auckland 2292/6375](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/6375)
- **Cadastral change event models (ISPRS).** "Event-based incremental updating of cadastral database" models land subdivision events (split, union, reallocation, modification of the common border) and uses an extended 4-intersection model for topological relations between parcels. A related paper separates low-level single-parcel change (appearance, expansion, deformation) from high-level topological change across several parcels (union, split, reallocation). France's cadastre recognises a fixed set of changes: division, merge, rectification, extraction, passage, reallocation, expropriation. **Relevance:** a ready-made event taxonomy and lineage schema. Each simulation event should store parent and child parcel IDs, which also gives a natural store for ghost boundaries. — [ISPRS XXXV comm2 229](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXV/congress/comm2/papers/229.pdf); [ISPRS XXXVII 253](https://isprs.org/proceedings/XXXVII/congress/4_pdf/253.pdf); [Spatio-temporal SOLAP, arXiv 1108.0831](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.0831)

### Inferences
- A workable design pairs a CHALMS/Filatova-style market, which decides *who owns* and *whether* to split or merge, with a cadastral-event geometry layer, which decides *how* the cut runs and *what lineage is recorded*. The ABMs found do not join these two layers at the polygon level.

### Gaps
- I did not search for Alexandridis's or Ettema's parcel models specifically, and found no sources. Remmert and Taubenböck on cadastral change also returned nothing. All are unverified and should not be cited without a follow-up search.

## 3. Empirical studies of long-run plot persistence, plot series, metrology and plot-pattern change

### Takeaway
The morphology literature agrees on several points. Plot patterns persist longer than buildings but less long than streets. Oversized blocks and plots are predictably broken down by many uncoordinated individual actions. Plot change is driven by *relations between neighbours* (Whitehand's neighbour effect) as much as by a plot's own traits. Metrological analysis shows that burgage series were often laid out at varied widths, not created by later subdivision of uniform units. That finding should shape how the default medieval initial layout is generated.

### Cited Findings
- **Siksna, A. (1997). "The evolution of block size and form in North American and Australian city centres." *Urban Morphology* 1(1).** Compares 12 city centres. Initial block size and form produce "predictable consequences" for later parcelling, building form and circulation. Over-sized blocks "are predictably broken down, through successive uncoordinated actions of individuals," giving more lot frontage and better circulation, and 50–70 m blocks perform best for pedestrian areas. **Relevance:** direct empirical targets for the 19th-century US scenario. Large initial grid blocks should be sliced by alleys and mid-block lanes over time. — [DOI 10.51347/jum.v1i1.4048](https://doi.org/10.51347/jum.v1i1.4048); [PDF](https://journal.urbanform.org/index.php/jum/article/download/4048/3359/13169)
- **Conzenian burgage cycle / plot metrology (Conzen; Slater; Whitehand).** The burgage cycle is the progressive filling of a burgage's back-land with buildings, ending in clearance and "urban fallow" before redevelopment. Slater used metrological analysis to reconstruct plot-boundary histories. Studies of Scottish burghs suggest plots "were set out to varying widths rather than the pattern resulting from later subdivision and amalgamation of plots of uniform width." **Relevance:** a temporal model for *buildings within plots* (back-land infill, then fallow), plus a caution against assuming uniform original perch-width plots. — [Whitehand-tradition review, Urban Morphology](https://journal.urbanform.org/index.php/jum/article/view/3896); [Burgage plot patterns in four Scottish burghs, PSAS](https://journals.socantscot.org/index.php/psas/article/view/9724)
- **Whitehand's neighbour effect hypothesis, and recent tests (Chalmers, Bobkova et al.).** Whitehand "proposed the overlooked neighbour effect hypothesis," which links change to the relational character of neighbouring properties. Recent work finds that "the propensity for change or persistence is generated by the degree of mutual support and interaction between neighbouring plots rather than their individual character." **Relevance:** change probabilities for split, merge or rebuild should depend on the state of neighbouring plots. This is a strong, citable rule for the simulation. — [Chalmers publication 513622](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/513622) (abstract via search; full page did not load); [Springer chapter 10.1007/978-3-031-77752-3_14 on plot configuration and spatial change](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-77752-3_14) (could not fetch, so authors are unconfirmed)
- **Bobkova, E. (PhD, Chalmers; and papers with Berghauser Pont, Marcus).** Plot systems connect physical form with institutions such as property rights. Develops quantitative plot measures and plot types, and tests the "spatial capacity" and burgage-cycle ideas against economic diversity. Prior studies show a correlation between the degree of land division and socio-economic diversity. **Relevance:** gives measures to check the output of the simulation (plot size, frontage, compactness distributions). — [Chalmers 252851](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/252851); [Chalmers 512548](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/?id=512548)
- **Kropf, K. "Plots, property and behaviour." *Urban Morphology* 22(1), 5–14** (the search listing said 2017; volume 22 is 2018). Clarifies "plot" vs "lot". Grounds the plot in property and control, which come from human behaviour, and discusses persistence and "differential rates of change between street pattern, plot pattern and building pattern." **Relevance:** conceptual basis for treating the plot as a control unit with its own change rate, between streets and buildings. — [Urban Morphology 4068](https://journal.urbanform.org/index.php/jum/article/view/4068)
- **Scheer, B. C. (2010). *The Evolution of Urban Form: Typology for Planners and Architects*. APA Planners Press / Routledge.** Scheer (2016) also writes on the "urban matrix" and stresses "the persistence of configurations such as street and plot patterns." She notes that the plot, though fundamental, is the least studied component. **Relevance:** typological framing of *why* fabric resists change. Gap: qualitative. — [Routledge listing](https://www.routledge.com/The-Evolution-of-Urban-Form-Typology-for-Planners-and-Architects/Scheer/p/book/9780367330071)
- **Moudon, A. V. (1986). *Built for Change: Neighborhood Architecture in San Francisco*. MIT Press.** A detailed study of how a 19th-century US grid neighbourhood changed over time. Its resilience comes from "small lot size, individual ownership, and easily adaptable room layouts." The book covers "gridiron, lots, and boxes." **Relevance:** the key empirical source for the 19th-century US scenario. Small, individually owned grid lots persist, and change happens at building scale. — [CiNii record](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA0039034X?l=en); [CCA library record](https://www.cca.qc.ca/en/search/details/library/publication/12314137)

### Inferences
- Simulation rule candidates with empirical backing: (a) neighbour-dependent change probability (Whitehand; Chalmers); (b) oversized blocks or plots get cut by lanes (Siksna); (c) in England, back-land infill comes before plot change (burgage cycle); (d) small individually owned lots resist amalgamation (Moudon). Amalgamation should need a multi-owner purchase, which links to the land-market ABMs in section 2.

### Gaps
- I did not find a citable quantitative rate (for example, the percentage of medieval plot boundaries surviving per century). Whitehand's specific studies of commercial plot amalgamation in town centres were not found by search. Follow up via the *Urban Morphology* archive and Whitehand & Whitehand-related studies of Birmingham or Ludlow.
- Sanders' work (if this means Sanders & Woodward on morphological plot change) was not located.

## 4. Land readjustment and informal-settlement subdivision

### Takeaway
Land readjustment and land consolidation have a well-developed *optimisation* literature for reallocating parcels (simulated annealing, metaheuristics). The informal-settlement morphology literature describes incremental "plot sectioning", inheritance division and consolidation. It is qualitative and lacks geometric algorithms.

### Cited Findings
- **Land reallocation optimisation (Turkey; KTUN / Erbakan repositories).** "Land Reallocation Model With Simulated Annealing Algorithm" reallocates blocks by scoring landowner requests. It finishes "in minutes, whereas the reallocation phase in traditional land consolidation projects typically takes weeks and months." Related work uses ABC, DE and PSO metaheuristics with parcel-based crossover operators. **Relevance:** a template for a "replanning" event, such as enclosure or a railroad company re-plat, that pools and redistributes plots. — [KTUN record](https://gcris.ktun.edu.tr/entities/publication/8e6c434c-fe5b-4cd0-90d6-7905abfbc5e2/full); [Geodesy & Cartography (Vilnius) article](https://journals.vilniustech.lt/index.php/GAC/article/download/2049/5047)
- **Informal-settlement morphogenesis.** Settlements change through "house expansion, plot sectioning (land subdivision), squatting, and inheritance land acquisition," and evolve "through subdivision, consolidation, building extension and functional intensification." Their floor area ratio rises over time. Morphological analysis "needs to be sensitive to the dynamics and the absence (or blurring) of physical boundaries." **Relevance:** inheritance-driven subdivision (splitting among heirs) is a historically plausible split trigger for medieval and early modern towns too. — [AAU thesis](https://etd.aau.edu.et/items/2634059d-08f0-4895-87a9-70858cb163e7/full); [Tessari & Alvim, ISUF 2021](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/80396/1/Tessari_Alvim_ISUF_2021_The_form_of_the_informal_a_type_morphological_analysis.pdf); [SAGE Open: Strengthening the morphological study of informal settlements](https://pollux-fid.de/r/cr-10.1177/2158244018760375)

### Inferences
- Inheritance splits (equal division among heirs, usually lengthwise to keep frontage access) and land-readjustment re-plats are two event types worth adding to the split/merge set.

### Gaps
- I found no geometric or procedural *algorithm* for informal self-built subdivision. The literature here is descriptive.

## 5. Robust polygon and geometry techniques cited in this literature

### Takeaway
Within the sources gathered, the cited geometric machinery is the straight skeleton (CityEngine; Kelly) and, in cadastral work, topological relations between parcels via an extended 4-intersection model. Topology-preserving planar-partition editing is implied by cadastral split/union events but was not explicitly researched.

### Cited Findings
- **Straight skeleton / mixed weighted straight skeleton** for street-aligned lots with guaranteed access. — [CityEngine docs](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/2019.0/help/help-layers-block-parameters.htm); [Kelly thesis](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/4975)
- **Extended 4-intersection topological model** for checking parcel relations during split and union updates. — [ISPRS event-based cadastral updating](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXV/congress/comm2/papers/229.pdf)

### Inferences
- From general knowledge, not verified in this session: standard robust polygon boolean references are Vatti (1992), *CACM* 35(7), which underlies the Clipper library, and Martínez, Rueda & Feito (2009), *Computers & Geosciences* 35(6), which underlies JS libraries such as polygon-clipping and martinez. A DCEL or shared-edge planar partition is the usual way to keep adjacent plots gap-free. Storing edges once, with tags and ghost flags, would carry frontage/side/back tags and ghost lines through splits and merges. Treat all of these as follow-ups, not verified citations.

### Gaps
- I did not verify a paper in the parcel literature that explicitly uses a DCEL or a topology-preserving planar partition. Vanegas 2012 (known) is the most likely place to look.
