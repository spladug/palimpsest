## Q1. English open-field morphology and origins (strips, furlongs, headlands, aratral curve, ridge and furrow, terrain/drainage)

### Takeaway
The core scholarship agrees on the hierarchy township > fields > furlongs > selions/strips (ridges), with headlands at furlong ends and the reverse-S (aratral) curve produced by turning the ox team. It disagrees on *when* and *why* regular open fields formed: 8th-9th c. (Oosthuizen), 9th-10th c. (Fox/Hall tradition), or 11th-12th c. in regular form (Williamson et al.). Furlong layout is strongly tied to soils, slope and drainage, which gives a simulation a principled way to orient strips on terrain.

### Inferences
- A generator can reasonably produce: township boundary -> 2-3 great fields -> furlongs whose strip direction follows downslope/drainage -> strips of ~1 furlong length with reverse-S ends and headlands at right angles; strips within a furlong are parallel and bundled.
- Because origin dates are contested, a sim can treat "regular open-field reorganisation" as a scenario event (8th-12th c.), not a fixed fact.

## Q2. Field/estate boundaries determining later urban street and plot layout

### Takeaway
Strong, long-established evidence exists at two scales: (a) medieval towns where burgage plots and back lanes were inserted into or inherited from open-field strips/furlongs (Conzen; Slater; Stratford, plot metrology), and (b) 18th-19th-century suburbs where the pre-urban cadastre (fields, closes, estate boundaries) controlled street lines and building types (Ward and Beresford on Leeds; Whitehand & Carr on 20th-c. suburbs). French work (Noizet/ALPAGE on Paris, Tours) treats this as "transmission/resilience of forms". US PLSS persistence is documented mainly in rural/suburban settings.

London: George Philip.** [V] PDF (FEUP/PNUM): https://vitoroliveira.fe.up.pt/alnwick.pdf — Founding text of town-plan analysis: plan units, burgage cycle, fringe belts, "morphological frame" whereby earlier boundaries persist and constrain later development. *Borrow:* burgage cycle (repletion -> climax -> clearance) as a plot-level state machine; fringe belts; the principle that plot boundaries are the most persistent plan element. ### Inferences
- The best-supported simulation rule: urbanisation consumes rural parcels as *units* (closes, furlong blocks, a landowner's strips), and new streets are drawn along, or parallel to, the longest boundaries of the parcel; burgage plots inherit strip width/orientation (~perch multiples), with back lanes on former headlands or furlong ends. This is consistent with Conzen, Slater and Ward though no single source states it as an algorithm.
- Headlands -> back lanes and furlong boundaries -> cross-streets is a plausible mapping often noted in the literature, but direct citable evidence was not retrieved in this session (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve a primary source specifically documenting burgage plots laid directly over ridge and furrow with surviving reverse-S plot boundaries in an English town (candidates from prior knowledge: Slater's Stratford/Lichfield work; Lilley's work on Coventry and Mapping Medieval Townscapes; excavations such as Newport, Dyfed, Medieval Archaeology 38 (1994), https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00766097.1994.11735566, which examined burgage plots later returned to agriculture). Recommend checking Slater (ed.) (2000) *Towns in Decline* / Lilley (2002) *Urban Life in the Middle Ages* [M].
- Reverse-S curves in urban plots (e.g. Nottingham, Leicester, London suburbs following strip curves) are widely cited anecdotally; no verified source captured here.

## Q3. Computational models: procedural field generation, ABMs of open-field formation, rural-to-urban boundary inheritance, cadastral reconstruction

### Takeaway
No published computational model was found that generates medieval open-field systems (furlongs/strips/aratral curves) and then inherits them as urban plots. Closest graphics work: Emilien et al. (village parcels by anisotropic conquest on terrain), Vanegas et al. (parcel subdivision persistent across edits), Lechner et al. (agent-based city/land use). Economic models of open fields (McCloskey) are analytical, not spatial ABMs. Agricultural "procedural field" papers are crop-row scenes for robotics, not land division. This is a genuine gap the project could fill.

### Cited Findings
17. **Emilien, A., Bernhardt, A., Peytavie, A., Cani, M.-P. and Galin, E. (2012). "Procedural generation of villages on arbitrary terrains." *The Visual Computer* 28(6-8): 809-818. ### Inferences
- A defensible pipeline with literature backing for each step: hydrology-based terrain (Génevaux) -> furlong blocks oriented by slope/drainage (Williamson/Liddiard) -> strip subdivision with aratral curves (Eyre, Hall) -> dispersed allocation to households (McCloskey) -> urban insertion consuming whole furlongs/closes, with OBB subdivision of plots along strip direction (Vanegas, Slater metrology) -> burgage cycle (Conzen) -> later suburban fill of closes (Ward, Beresford).

### Gaps
- No ABM of open-field *formation* (e.g. from partible division + periodic reallocation) located in JASSS/CoMSES within this session's search budget.
- No computational work found on "rural-to-urban boundary inheritance" as such; historical GIS (ALPAGE) and cadastral vectorisation (below) provide data but not generative models.

## Q4. Land fragmentation under inheritance systems (partible vs primogeniture), and cadastral reconstruction

### Takeaway
Simple stochastic models of equal division among a Poisson number of sons reproduce realistic plot-size distributions; GIS work (Demetriou) quantifies fragmentation and models re-parcelling for consolidation. Historic cadastre vectorisation (EPFL/Venice, Lausanne) is mature and could supply validation data.

### Cited Findings
23. **"The distribution of landed property" (arXiv:0901.0765, 2009).** [V existence/title; authors and venue not confirmed — fetch failed] https://arxiv.org/pdf/0901.0765 — Agent-based model of plot-size distributions under partible inheritance (equal division among sons; sons per generation Poisson-distributed) plus land trading, yielding a steady-state distribution; contrasts with primogeniture. *Borrow:* a minimal, directly implementable inheritance/subdivision rule for strips and burgages (split strips lengthwise among heirs).

LandFragmentS / LandParcelS papers (e.g. FIG Working Week 2012 paper; *Land Use Policy* and *Computers, Environment and Urban Systems* articles).** [V project and FIG paper] https://fig.net/resources/proceedings/fig_proceedings/fig2012/papers/ts09e/TS09E_demetriou_stillwell_et_al_5626.pdf ; White Rose: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/80439 — GIS + multi-attribute decision model producing a global land fragmentation index (six factors) for Cyprus, and LandParcelS, an automated land partitioning (re-parcelling) module within the LACONISS consolidation system. *Borrow:* fragmentation metrics to score simulation output; automated constrained partitioning algorithm. *Gap:* not about historic inheritance dynamics.

- Classic historical empirical work on partible inheritance and fragmentation (Thirsk, J. (1961) "Industries in the countryside" in Fisher (ed.) *Essays in the Economic and Social History of Tudor and Stuart England*; Homans (1941) *English Villagers of the Thirteenth Century*; Kentish gavelkind) [M] — not verified this session.

### Inferences
- For medieval England default: villein holdings (virgates/yardlands) were usually impartible under manorial custom in Midland areas, while partible custom (gavelkind in Kent, parts of East Anglia) produced finer fragmentation [M]; the sim can expose inheritance custom as a regional parameter that drives strip subdivision rates.

### Gaps
- No verified JASSS/Land Use Policy ABM specifically on historical partible-inheritance fragmentation; recommend searching CoMSES (https://catalog.comses.net) for "inheritance" models.
- Authorship of arXiv:0901.0765 not confirmed.
